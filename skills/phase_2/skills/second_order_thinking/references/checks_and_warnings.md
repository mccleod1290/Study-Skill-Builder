# Checks and warnings

Read at step 6 of `SKILL.md`, when you use the word iatrogenic, or when a later effect looks too clean. Tags are in `SKILL.md`. Source numbers are in `references/sources.md`.

## Hardin: three checks, used together

[4] g5: Hardin's talk. The grade is for what he says. The talk names three levels of education: literacy, numeracy, ecolacy. It does not call them filters. The word filter appears once in the talk, in the title of the book Filters Against Folly (g5). The blog [5] and the publisher text [15] call them filters (g3).

The talk words the three questions: appropriate word; how much, how many; and then what ([4] g5). Other sources word them differently. The wording in the book is an open question (g1). Quote the wording of the talk as the talk's. Do not quote another wording as Hardin's.

| check | ask | what Hardin says [4] g5 | how this skill uses it (`rule:`) |
|---|---|---|---|
| literacy | what is the appropriate word? | A person who is only literate lets vague, grand phrases pass (the sea's inexhaustible wealth). He checks loaded words such as shortage and foreign aid. A nation is an abstraction. It has no voice. Only its citizens have voices. | Name the actor in each effect. Say what happens, not "secure" or "safe". Watch for words that hide who acts: `reading g2`. [5] g3: the blog says Hardin calls this the literate tendency to replace actors with abstractions. The book was not opened. |
| numeracy | how much, how many? | Skill with numbers, plus the habit of insisting on them. Ask for amounts and rates. Graphs help. A growth rate of 3 percent a year is about nineteenfold in a century. | Size each later effect against the first. A later effect you cannot size is a guess: g2. It feeds stop test 2. |
| ecolacy | and then what? | Practical grasp of how each quasi-stable state gives way to another over time. The world is a web of linked systems, so we can rarely be sure an intervention gives the result we want. An action never has only one effect. | Steps 3 to 5 of `SKILL.md`. |

More from [4] g5:

- Advice rests on our best opinion and on incomplete knowledge. He lists three ways to cut the cost of error: history and other cultures, tests at pilot scale, and theory. Use (`reading g2`): when a later effect is a guess, a pilot or a history check is the cheapest way to lift it above g2.
- He uses the three levels to argue positions on foreign aid, food banks and population control. Take the method. Do not take the positions.

Limits:

- A filter can show that a claim is weak. It cannot show that the opposite claim is true: `reading g2`.
- The question has no end of its own. [4] g5: in the ecolate view time has no end point. Whether the book bounds it is an open question (g1). The stop rule in `SKILL.md` is this skill's own.
- The talk page has transcription slips. Do not trust it for exact wording. His aphorism that an action never has only one effect is in print with two word orders (g3). Do not quote it as exact.

## Iatrogenics: a warning, not a rule

Sources: [6] the Farnam Street post (secondary), [8] MeSH (official).

| point | what the source says | tag |
|---|---|---|
| meaning in [6] | An intervention whose harm exceeds its gain. The healer can be anyone who intervenes: a chief executive, a state, an alliance of willing parties. | [6] g5 (says) |
| meaning in [8] | An adverse condition a patient develops because of treatment by a doctor, a surgeon or another health professional. The record defines the term. It states no warning to count harm. | [8] g5, for the medical definition only |
| the gap | [6] adds a net-harm test and widens the intervener. With clinicians, use the [8] sense. Use the [6] sense only after you say so. | g4 ([6] and [8] joined) |
| the lesson in [6] | Know both the benefit and the harm of an intervention, including second and later order effects. Without both you cannot tell when you do more harm than good. [6] names intervening without that knowledge naive interventionism and credits Taleb with the term. | [6] g5 (says) |
| why harm hides | Harm is easy to tie to an intervention when it is immediate, visible and direct. Delayed harm, or harm with several possible causes, is less likely to be tied to it. | [6] g5 (says) |
| why people keep intervening | They miss second and later order effects. They sit far from the consequences. They have a bias for action. They have no skin in the game. | [6] g5 (says) |

Use: write the 10-month and 10-year probes before you trust a good first result. Ask who carries the later cost.

Limits:

- [6] is a blog. For claims about the world it stops at g3. It cites Taleb, Chernow and Munger without a page. None was opened.
- [6] g5 (says), g2 (true): benefits should exceed harm by orders of magnitude. This is the author's judgement, not verified as the right bar. His lean toward doing nothing is also his judgement. It allows action when the gain is clear. Merton gives no do-nothing rule (g4). Iatrogenics is a warning to count harm. It is not a reason to do nothing.
- Iatrogenics is a subset of Merton's field, not a synonym (g4). Merton counts unforeseen results that are good, neutral or bad. Iatrogenics needs harm above gain.
- [6] credits Hippocrates with "first do no harm". A paper abstract [14] reports that neither Hippocrates nor Galen wrote it. The sources conflict (g1, open question; abstract only). Do not state the credit as fact.

## Merton: why a later effect goes unanticipated

[7] g5: Merton's paper. The grade is for what he says. It is a 1936 theory essay with no data. The copy is a scan with OCR text. The paper's term is "unanticipated". It names four factors and one other circumstance. It does not number five causes (g4). Later summaries do [12][13], g3. Do not write "five causes".

| factor | what Merton says | ask in a chain |
|---|---|---|
| ignorance | Knowledge of human conduct is stochastic: a repeated act has a range of results. Chance effects come from too many forces to predict. Life forces action on opinion and estimate, and forecasting costs time and energy. There are limits to waiting for certainty. | Which later effect can nobody know yet? |
| error | Error can enter at four steps: judging the present, inferring the future, choosing the action, carrying it out. A common error is to assume that what worked before will work again. Habit makes it automatic. Merton says error is perhaps as pervasive as ignorance. | Did the chain assume the past repeats? |
| imperious immediacy of interest | The actor's paramount concern with the foreseen near-term result excludes the further effects of the same act. Intense interest often tends to preclude the analysis it needs. | Who wanted the first order so much that they stopped there? |
| basic values | The actor skips the consequences because his values demand the act. Weber's case: active asceticism produces wealth, and wealth erodes asceticism. | Does a value demand the act, so the later effect is set aside? |
| one other circumstance | A forecast made public becomes part of the situation, so the forecast may fail. | Does saying the forecast change the thing forecast? |

More from [7] g5:

- Unforeseen results are not always undesirable. A later effect can be good.
- The factors are only factors. None explains a concrete case alone.
- Saying we would have foreseen the result with more knowledge is a weak reason. It is circular, or it overweights one factor.
- It is not warranted to say that all social planning can work, or that none can. The paper gives no do-nothing rule (g4).

Keep two lists apart. Merton's factors explain why results go unanticipated. The four reasons in [6] explain why people keep intervening when harm exceeds gain (g4). Do not merge them.

Use: name the factor in the lesson cell when it explains the miss. Do not borrow labels, glosses or examples from [12] or [13]. They add things the paper does not have (g3).

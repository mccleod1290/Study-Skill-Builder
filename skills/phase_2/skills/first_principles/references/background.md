# Background: what first principles are, what each tool costs, two cautions

Read this when you explain what first principles are, when you give an example of a kind of part, when you weigh analogy against parts, when you write a gate row, or when the Aristotle question comes up. Source numbers and marks are in `references/sources.md` and in `SKILL.md`.

## What first principles are

The FS page says first principles thinking is not necessarily a search for absolute truths. It looks for the elements that are irreducible in a given situation [1 C04 g5].

The page's intro, however, splits what we know is absolutely true from what we only assume [1 C02 g5]. Read the intro's wording with the qualifier in The Basics (house).

First principles are not a fixed list. Our grasp of them grows as we learn, and they differ from one situation to the next [1 C05 g5]. Urban's gloss: in science every given holds for now and can change [2 U2 g5].

One case. For a fridge's efficiency, the laws of thermodynamics serve as first principles. A physicist may go deeper and question the assumptions under them [1 C06 g5]. So first principles belong to a person and a context. Write both (derived from [1 C05 C06], g4).

## The three kinds of part, with examples from the notes

- Law: the laws of thermodynamics in the fridge case above [1 C06 g5]. A law holds whatever anyone believes. Our reading of it can still move [1 C05 C06 g5].
- Convention: money and borders [1 C07 g5]. "Rockets were costly, so they stay costly" [2 R1 g5].
- Assumption: "we must move first" [1 C24 g5]. The page's rebuttal names no evidence [1 W5 g3].

## Analogy and parts: what each costs

Analogy has a use. It makes a hard problem easier to explain and understand. It has costs. It limits what you think is possible. It lets an argument stand without exposing faulty thinking. It makes you see the problem as someone else does [1 C26 g5].

Relying on other people's analogies is incremental thinking [1 C26 g5].

Musk, as the FS page quotes him, says most people reason by analogy because it is mentally easier. He says first principles takes a lot more mental energy [1 C27 g5 as said; g3 as fact. Page rechecked 2026-11-21].

Parts work has costs too. In Urban's model, even a chef runs on autopilot on a normal day. The work drains mental energy and is usually not worth the time. It is for the day when something new must be decided [2 L1 g5]. It promises no good result [2 L2 g5]. The chef examples Urban lists are all successes [2 L5 g5].

The FS page tells success stories only [1 L06 g5]. It names more than one cost. One is effort ("a lot of work"). The why chain also feels slow, and it brings a defensive reaction when you find you do not know [1 C08 C14 g5. Page rechecked 2026-11-21. The last sentence of notes line L06 is wrong].

## Two cautions

A false claim can sit among your first principles. Flood geologists took an Earth 6,000 years old as a first principle. A chain built on it has no chance of reaching truth [2 W3 g5]. Check where your chain stops.

Aristotle's first principles are another idea. They must be true and cannot be demonstrated [13 A3 A4 g5]. Do not merge the two.

- The FS page places the idea in a line from Plato, with contributions from Aristotle and Descartes. It then says its own use is less grand, as above [1 The Basics g5; not in the notes].
- The page's source list names Aristotle, Physics 184a10-21 and Metaphysics 1013a14-15. The text carries no marker for them [1 L03 g5. Rechecked 2026-11-21].
- The FS page does not tie Musk's phrase to Aristotle [1 whole page g5, checked 2026-11-21; not in the notes]. Urban never names Aristotle [2 J4 g5].
- Whether Musk's use of the phrase comes from Aristotle is an open question. The sources opened do not say [2 13 J5 g1].
